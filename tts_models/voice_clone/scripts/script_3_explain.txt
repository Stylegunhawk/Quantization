# SCRIPT 3 of 3 — Explaining, narrating, reacting
# Runs ~12 minutes at the pace measured on script 1 (150 wpm of speech).
# Same room, same mic, same distance as scripts 1 and 2.
#
# HOW TO RECORD
#   - TURN VOICE ISOLATION OFF. Control Centre > Mic Mode > Standard.
#     Measured twice on your own voice: it costs 23.2pp of speaker identity
#     (98.7% -> 75.5% on identical passages), because it strips sub-300Hz chest
#     resonance. Worse for training, it punches hard digital-zero holes INSIDE
#     sentences -- 44 interior gaps, median 223ms, 19.7% of clip duration --
#     erasing the breaths and plosive stops the model must learn pause structure
#     from. It does not hurt transcription at all, which is why it looks safe.
#   - Voice Memos, one continuous file, ~1s between passages.
#   - Fumbled a line? Pause two seconds and redo that passage from its
#     start. The aligner keeps the better take and drops the other.
#
# This one covers what the other two miss: explaining something step by step,
# telling a short story with a beginning and an end, reading numbers and dates
# out loud, and a few passages with genuine emotional colour. Those intonation
# patterns are the hardest for a model to invent, so they have to be in the data.
#
# Read the numbers naturally, the way you would say them, not digit by digit.

1. Let me try to explain how this actually works, because I had to have it explained to me twice.

2. You start with a recording. The model breaks the sound into small pieces, about twelve of them per second.

3. Each piece becomes a number, and the whole recording becomes a long list of numbers.

4. Then the model learns to predict the next number from the ones before it, which is the same trick language models use for words.

5. When you want new speech, you give it some text, and it produces a new list of numbers, and something else turns those back into sound.

6. That last part is the piece people forget. Predicting the numbers is only half of it.

7. The reason it needs so many examples is that it has to learn how your voice behaves in situations it has never heard.

8. If you only ever record yourself saying calm sentences, it will not know what you sound like asking a question.

9. That is why these scripts have questions in them. Have you noticed how many? There have been four so far.

10. Emphasis is another one. When I say I did not say that, it means something different from when I say I did not say that.

11. Same eight words, completely different meaning, and the only difference is which one I lean on.

12. A model that has never heard you emphasise anything will produce speech that is technically correct and sounds dead.

13. Here is a story. About three years ago I lost a week of work because of a single character.

14. I had written a script that cleaned up a folder of temporary files. It worked perfectly for about two months.

15. Then one day the variable holding the folder name was empty, and the script cheerfully deleted everything from the root instead.

16. I watched it happen. There is a particular feeling when you realise what a progress bar is actually measuring.

17. I had backups, which is the only reason this is a funny story instead of a career-ending one.

18. The restore took about six hours and I did not sleep much that night.

19. The fix was one line. Check that the variable is not empty before you use it.

20. I now check that variable in every script I write, and I will do it for the rest of my life.

21. That is the thing about painful lessons. They are extremely effective and nobody would choose to learn that way.

22. Here is a smaller one. Last year I spent an entire afternoon convinced a function was broken.

23. I added print statements everywhere. I read the documentation twice. I asked two people.

24. It turned out I was editing one file and running a different one, in a folder I had copied a week earlier.

25. Four hours. For a wrong path. I did not tell anyone for about a month.

43. Back to explaining, because that is most of what I actually do with my voice.

44. The way I would describe it to someone with no background is this. Imagine teaching someone to imitate you.

45. First you play them a short clip. They will get the general shape of it, roughly half right.

46. That is what we tried first, and half right is genuinely what it sounded like.

47. Then imagine instead that they spend an hour listening to you talk about all sorts of things.

48. They start to pick up the small things. Where you pause. How you end a question. Which sounds you shorten.

49. That is what this recording is for. Not one clip, but enough material for the pattern to show up.

50. There is a threshold somewhere. Below it, the model memorises the exact sentences and produces noise on anything new.

51. Above it, it starts to generalise, and that is when it becomes a voice instead of a recording.

52. We were well below that threshold on the first attempt, which is why the result was worse than a generic voice.

53. That was not a failure of the method. It was a failure to feed it enough, and those are different problems.

54. Something I find genuinely interesting is that the model does not store your voice as a sound.

55. It stores it as a set of tendencies. How long you hold a vowel, how much your pitch moves, what you do at the end of a sentence.

56. Which means it can say things you have never said, in a voice that is recognisably yours, without ever having heard those words from you.

57. That is either impressive or unsettling depending on the day you have had.

58. There is an obvious question about consent that sits underneath all of this.

59. Recording your own voice for your own model is straightforward. Everything past that gets complicated quickly.

60. I think the honest position is that the technology is ahead of the norms, and it will stay ahead for a while.

61. Let me describe the room I am in, because descriptive speech has a different rhythm from explaining.

62. There is a window on the left, a desk against the wall, and a lamp that I have never once moved.

63. The chair is slightly too low and I keep meaning to adjust it and I never do.

64. There are three cables on the floor that go somewhere, and I could not tell you where.

65. Outside there is a road, and beyond that a building with a red sign that is lit at night.

66. I have looked at that sign for four years and I have never read what it says.

67. There is something about the things you see every day. You stop actually seeing them.

68. I noticed a crack in the ceiling last month that has probably been there since before I moved in.

69. Now some longer sentences, because sentence length affects how the model learns to breathe.

70. When I first started working on this, I assumed the difficult part would be the modelling, but it turned out that almost every problem was about data, or memory, or the gap between what a script claimed to do and what it actually did.

71. The measurement is more important than the change, because a change you cannot measure is just an opinion with extra steps, and I have shipped enough opinions to know how that ends.

72. If you had asked me a month ago how much audio a system like this needs, I would have guessed about two minutes, and I would have been wrong by a factor of somewhere between ten and twenty.

77. Short sentences and long ones use completely different breathing, and a model trained only on one will get the other wrong.

78. Here is the last section. A few things I have changed my mind about.

79. I used to think documentation was for other people. It is mostly for you, six months later, and you will not remember.

80. I used to think the clever solution was the good one. Now I think the boring one is, almost always.

81. I used to think that being fast mattered most. It turns out that being correct and being clear matter more, and speed follows from both.

82. I used to think you should finish everything you start. I now abandon things much earlier and I am happier for it.

83. And I used to think that the hard part of learning something was the concepts. It is not. It is the repetition.

85. Which is roughly twenty times what we had the first time, and that is the whole point of the exercise.
