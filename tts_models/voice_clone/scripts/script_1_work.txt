1. I have been working on speech models for a few weeks now, mostly in the evenings after everything else is done.

2. The setup is simple enough. One model stays in memory at a time, and I measure how long each one takes before I trust any of the numbers.

3. Most of the interesting problems turn out to be about memory rather than raw speed.

4. When something looks wrong, I read the log first. Then I check the measurement itself, before I change a single line of code.

5. That habit has saved me a lot of time, and it has also saved me from fixing things that were never broken.

6. Yesterday I spent about two hours chasing a bug that turned out to be a stale cache. The code was fine the whole time.

7. There is a particular kind of frustration in that. You want the problem to be interesting, and instead it is just old data.

8. I keep a note file open while I work. Anything surprising goes in it, with the date, because I will not remember the details next week.

9. The laptop has eight gigabytes of memory, which sounds small until you realise how much you can fit if you are careful.

10. Being careful mostly means loading one thing at a time and actually freeing it when you are done.

11. I test on the smallest example that still fails. If it passes on the small one, the bug is somewhere in the setup, not the logic.

12. A colleague asked me why I bother measuring everything. The honest answer is that I have been wrong too often to trust my intuition about speed.

13. Benchmarks lie in both directions. Sometimes the first run is slow because nothing is warmed up, and sometimes it is fast because the data is already cached.

14. So I run it three times and take the middle number. It is not sophisticated, but it stops me from reporting noise.

15. The first version of anything I write is usually too clever. I go back the next morning and make it boring.

16. Boring code is easier to read at three in the morning when something has gone wrong in production.

17. I have started writing down what I expect to happen before I run something. When the result disagrees, one of us is wrong, and it is useful to know which.

18. Documentation is the part everyone skips, including me, until the day you need to remember why a decision was made.

19. There is a comment in one of my files that just says, do not change this, and I no longer remember what it was protecting.

20. That is a lesson about comments. Write down the reason, not the instruction.

21. Meetings are better when someone has written the agenda down beforehand. Even three lines is enough.

22. I try to send a short summary afterwards. Two or three sentences, what we decided and who is doing what.

23. Half the confusion on any project comes from people having slightly different pictures of the same plan in their heads.

24. Writing it down forces the differences into the open, which is uncomfortable at first and useful immediately after.

25. I reviewed a pull request this morning that changed four hundred lines. I asked if it could be split into three smaller ones.

26. Small changes get reviewed properly. Large ones get approved with a thumbs up and nobody has actually read them.

27. The best review comment I ever received was a question, not a correction. It asked what happens if the list is empty.

28. It was empty, of course. It had been empty in testing for a month and nobody had noticed.

29. Tests should check what the code does, not how it does it. Otherwise every refactor breaks the suite for no good reason.

30. I have written tests that were so tightly coupled to the implementation that changing a variable name broke twelve of them.

31. That is not a safety net. That is a tripwire.

32. The database question comes up on every project. Someone wants to add a new table, someone else wants to add a column, and both are defensible.

33. Usually the right answer depends on how the data will be read, not how it will be written.

34. Reads outnumber writes by a wide margin in almost everything I have worked on.

35. Caching is the classic example of a fix that creates two new problems. Now you have stale data and an invalidation strategy.

36. I try to avoid a cache until I have measured that the thing is actually slow.

37. Often it is not slow. It just felt slow because I was watching it.

38. There is a difference between latency and throughput that people mix up constantly, including me, when I am tired.

39. One is how long a single thing takes. The other is how many things finish in a given period. You can improve one and ruin the other.

40. Queues are the same. Adding workers helps until the bottleneck moves somewhere else, usually the database.

41. I read a post last week arguing that most systems are not big enough to need the architecture they have.

42. I think that is right more often than people want to admit. Complexity has a cost that shows up later, on someone else's shift.

43. The simplest thing that works is usually the right starting point. You can always add the complicated version once you know you need it.

44. Going the other direction, removing complexity once it is in, is much harder. Everything ends up depending on it.

45. I deleted about six hundred lines of code last month and nothing broke. That was a good day.

46. Deleting code is underrated. It is the only change that cannot introduce a new bug in the thing you deleted.

47. Version control saves you from your own confidence. I commit early and often, even when the work is half finished.

48. Branch names matter more than people think. Coming back to something called temp fix two after a week is genuinely painful.

49. I try to write the commit message as if a stranger will read it, because in six months that stranger is me.

50. Continuous integration is one of those things that feels like overhead until the first time it catches something before a release.

51. Then you never want to work without it again.

52. Deployment should be boring. If deploying is exciting, something is wrong with the process.

53. The best deployments I have been part of were the ones nobody noticed, including the people who did them.

54. Rolling back should be as easy as rolling forward. That is the part teams forget until they need it at two in the morning.

55. Monitoring tells you something is wrong. Logging tells you what. You need both and they answer different questions.

56. An alert that fires constantly is not an alert, it is background noise, and people learn to ignore it within a week.

57. I would rather have three alerts that always matter than thirty that mostly do not.

58. Error messages are a user interface. A message that says something went wrong is not helping anyone.

59. Say what failed, what was expected, and what to try next. Three pieces of information, and most messages contain none of them.

60. I have started reading the source of libraries I depend on. Not all of it, just the part I am actually calling.

61. It takes twenty minutes and it removes a surprising amount of guesswork.

62. Dependencies are borrowed complexity. You did not write it, but you still own the behaviour when it breaks.

63. Every package you add is a decision you will have to maintain, upgrade, and eventually justify.

64. Security is the area where being lazy costs the most. Validate the input at the boundary, every time, no exceptions.

65. Nobody has ever regretted checking the input too carefully.

66. Performance work should start with a measurement and end with a measurement. Anything in between is guessing.

67. I have optimised the wrong function more than once. It felt productive right up until the total time did not move.

68. Learning something properly takes longer than it looks, and skipping the boring foundation always costs more later.

69. I have restarted the same tutorial three times and I think that is fine. Some things need a few passes.

70. The part I enjoy most is the moment where a thing that made no sense suddenly does, and you cannot remember why it was confusing.

71. Someone asked me last week how I decide what to work on first. Mostly I ask which thing I would be embarrassed to explain if it broke.

72. That question sorts a backlog faster than any scoring system I have tried.

73. Estimates are guesses wearing a suit. I have stopped pretending otherwise and started giving ranges instead.

74. A range is honest. It says here is the good case, here is the bad one, and here is what would push it toward the bad one.

75. The hardest part of any project is not the code. It is agreeing on what the thing is supposed to do.

76. I have watched two people argue for forty minutes before realising they agreed completely and were using one word differently.

77. Naming is genuinely hard, and not in a funny way. A bad name spreads through a codebase and outlives everyone who touched it.

78. I renamed a function last month and found nineteen places where the old name had leaked into comments and variable names.

79. Refactoring is easiest when the tests are good and terrifying when they are not.

80. There is a specific fear that comes from changing code with no test coverage. You are not editing, you are gambling.

81. I try to add one test before I change anything, just to have a heartbeat monitor while I work.

82. Reading other people's code is a skill separate from writing it, and I do not practise it nearly enough.

83. Configuration is where simple projects go to become complicated. Every option is a branch that someone has to reason about.

84. I have started defaulting aggressively and only exposing a setting when someone actually asks for it twice.

85. Logging too much is almost as bad as logging nothing. You end up with a haystack and no needle.
